The Setup

The cleanest way to frame this game is as a collision between two teams whose strengths show up in different columns of the box score. Duke wins the columns that decide the rhythm of a football game: rushing yards, time of possession, points per game. North Carolina wins the columns that are harder to fake over a season: penalties, explosive plays allowed, strength of schedule. Duke averages 226.6 rushing yards and holds the ball for 33:40 a game, eighth nationally. UNC averages 112.4 rushing yards and holds it for 26:40, 132nd. One team wants to play 70 snaps. The other has spent five weeks trying to win in about 55.

The stakes are real on both sides. UNC is coming off its first win over a ranked opponent in the Bill Belichick era, a 21-17 road win at No. 25 Pitt, but it has yet to string together consecutive wins over FBS opponents this season and has not held the Victory Bell since 2023. Duke has lost its first game of the year, 28-25 at Georgia Tech, on a missed 33-yard field goal with eight seconds left, and now faces the possibility of back-to-back ACC losses with a trip to Virginia on deck. Nine ACC teams have one league loss and five have two. A second would leave Duke's title hopes on life support, which is why this is effectively a must-win for the Blue Devils. UNC, for its part, hosts No. 3 Miami on October 31, so there is a real fork in the road coming for the Tar Heels' season regardless of what happens Saturday.

The rivalry history leans toward tension rather than comfort. Duke has won the last two meetings, 21-20 at Wallace Wade in 2024 after UNC led 20-0, and 32-25 at Kenan Stadium last November on a fake field goal that set up the go-ahead touchdown with 2:18 left. UNC had won five straight before that. The last four games, split evenly, have been decided by a combined 13 points. Manny Diaz is 2-0 against UNC as Duke's coach, though he was 0-3 against the Tar Heels in three years at Miami. UNC leads the all-time series by more than 20 games, though the exact tally is disputed because of an 1889 forfeit and two vacated UNC wins, and Duke has had the better of it recently.

North Carolina: The Team

Year two of the Belichick era has been a different animal from year one, which ended 4-8 (2-6 ACC) and included a 48-14 loss to TCU. The Tar Heels have a new offensive coordinator in Bobby Petrino, who replaced Freddie Kitchens and runs what the program describes as a "veer and shoot" scheme. They have a new starting quarterback in senior Billy Edwards Jr. A largely rebuilt roster, with transfers at tight end, offensive line, linebacker and kicker. And an unusual situation on defense: coordinator Steve Belichick has been on medical leave since August, so Bill Belichick has been calling the defense himself, the first time he has done so full-time since 2019.

The results: a 15-10 win over TCU in Dublin as a 9.5-point underdog at some books, a 35-3 win over FCS East Tennessee State, a 28-20 loss at Clemson after leading 17-6 at the half (and after a weather delay of more than two hours in the fourth quarter), a 37-26 home loss to then-No. 3 Notre Dame, and the 21-17 win at Pitt. That is five games against an unusually tough slate. UNC's strength of schedule ranks 10th nationally, and two of the five opponents (Notre Dame and Pitt) were ranked when UNC played them.

The per-game profile is lopsided in an interesting way. UNC scores 23.4 points a game and allows 19.0. It throws for 218.2 yards and runs for 112.4, which makes this a passing offense that happens to have a running game rather than the other way around. The defense allows 204.2 passing yards and 152.0 rushing yards a game, and that split is the whole story of the defense: good against the pass, soft against the run, and getting softer as games go on. UNC has allowed each of its last three opponents at least 150 rushing yards, and Notre Dame piled up 241 on 45 carries, with 122 yards on 20 carries in the third quarter alone. Nationally, UNC ranks 98th in rushing offense and 88th in rushing defense.

The advanced numbers are kinder to the defense than the box score. By advanced metrics, UNC is inside the top 45 nationally in yards per play allowed, PPA per play and explosiveness allowed, and it is among the most disciplined teams in the country, ranking second in penalties per game and tied for fifth in fewest penalties. The flip side is ball security and sack avoidance. UNC has surrendered the ball eight times, and Edwards has been sacked 11 times. Four of Edwards' five interceptions have come in the last two games.

Strip out the FCS game and the picture tightens. Against four FBS opponents, UNC has scored 82 points (20.5 a game) and allowed 92 (23.0). That is the profile of a team that is hanging with good opponents more than beating them, which is roughly what the 3-2 record says.

Duke: The Team

Diaz is in year three at Duke with a record of 18-9 through two seasons, nine wins each year, an ACC title last December (an overtime win over Virginia in Charlotte) and a Sun Bowl win over Arizona State. In April the school extended him through 2031. He did it in 2025 with an offense built around quarterback Darian Mensah, who has since left through the portal, and he lost a significant share of the roster beyond that, including cornerback Chandler Rivers and safeties Terry Moore and Caleb Weaver. Nobody expected this team to be 4-0, which makes the 4-1 start easier to appreciate and the Georgia Tech loss easier to forgive.

The numbers are better than UNC's across the board. Duke scores 34.0 points a game (48th nationally) and allows 14.4 (13th). It rushes for 226.6 yards, passes for 230.0, allows 84.0 on the ground (sixth nationally, third in the ACC) and 202.0 through the air. The results: a 17-3 win over Tulane, a 31-27 win at Illinois, a 35-7 win over Stanford, a 62-7 win over FCS William & Mary, and the 28-25 loss at Georgia Tech. The rest of this section is about why that 34.0 should be handled with care, because the William & Mary game accounts for a large share of it. Remove it and Duke's four-game FBS line is 27.0 points scored and 16.25 allowed, still strong but not overwhelming, and Duke's strength of schedule ranks outside the top 90.

Duke's defining trait is its second-half defense. Through four games the Blue Devils outscored opponents 62-6 after halftime, which means they allowed just six second-half points all season before Georgia Tech scored 14 in the final two quarters on Saturday. That means roughly 38 of the 44 points Duke allowed in its first four games came before halftime. Diaz's staff adjusts well. UNC should treat the first half as a window, and keep that in mind for the game plan later.

Duke's defining flaw is discipline. The Blue Devils rank 122nd nationally in fewest penalties and 126th in penalties per game, and they were flagged six times for 71 yards at Georgia Tech, including a late hit on a 2nd-and-20 that handed the Yellow Jackets a first down on the drive that produced the winning touchdown. Duke is also slow, 114th in seconds per play, which compresses the game and shrinks the number of possessions. On the injury front, Duke lost starting kicker Cosme Salas and linebacker Elliott Schaper for the season this week in what Diaz called "freak" practice accidents, and running back Nate Sheppard, who played through an ankle injury at Georgia Tech that has been described as limiting his explosiveness, is listed as questionable with an undisclosed injury on Wednesday's initial ACC availability report. North Carolina's own report is longer and more damaging, and it gets its own section below.

The ACC's initial availability reports for Saturday came out Wednesday night, and they are worse for North Carolina than anything I expected coming into the week. UNC listed 17 players. Six are out: cornerback Tre Miller, who intercepted a pass at Pitt and whose injury may be season-ending; defensive back Jakob Weatherspoon; receiver Keeyun Chapman; and defensive linemen Viliami Moala, Vodney Cleveland and Jimmy-Phrisco Alo-Suliafu. Nickel corner Kaleb Cost, hurt at Pitt, is doubtful. Safety Greg Smith III, who leads the team in interceptions and appears on the report for the first time this season, is questionable, along with defensive lineman Isaiah Johnson, left tackle Jordan Hall, tight end Jordan Washington (who has yet to make his UNC debut), receiver Mason Humphrey and defensive lineman Donovan Hoilette Jr. Defensive lineman Jaylen Harvey, safety Peyton Waters, receiver Kymistrii Young and tight end Deems May are probable.

Duke's list is 12 players long. Three are questionable: Sheppard (undisclosed), interior lineman Matt Craycraft and defensive back Evan Smith, who has the team's only interception. Nine are out: kicker Cosme Salas, linebacker Elliott Schaper, quarterback Lawrence Gardner, receivers Jamien Little, Blake Rohrer and Qeanu Johnson, and linemen Kai Jacobowitz, Reagan McCranie and Roman Fina. Salas and Schaper are the two significant losses, and both were season-ending.

The practical read is that the injuries cluster on UNC's back end. A Tar Heel secondary that was the steadier of the two on Monday could be without its best young corner, its nickel and, if Smith sits, its best ball-hawk, against an offense that throws play-action off the league's best run game. The defensive line has lost three bodies behind Lewis, and a missing left tackle would matter against a Duke front with 11 sacks. Duke's best news is that its list is mostly depth pieces, and its biggest question mark is the one that matters most, Sheppard. Here is what I am hearing. For North Carolina, the probable players (Harvey, Waters, Young and May) will play and the doubtful Cost will not. Safety Greg Smith III and lineman Donovan Hoilette Jr. are expected to play, and the other questionable names (Hall, Johnson, Washington and Humphrey) are game-time decisions that will not be settled until the final report or pregame warmups. Smith playing matters most, because it keeps the Tar Heels' one proven ball-hawk on the field. For Duke, all three questionable players are expected to play: Sheppard, Craycraft and Evan Smith. Under ACC policy, probable means roughly a 75 percent chance of playing, questionable about 50 percent and doubtful about 25 percent. Updated reports are due each night at 8 p.m. ET, and the final report comes two hours before kickoff, at 1:30 p.m. ET Saturday.

North CarolinaBilly Edwards Jr. is a senior, formerly of Maryland and Wisconsin, who beat out the competition after Gio Lopez left for Wake Forest. He is 79 of 132 (59.8 percent) for 1,069 yards, eight touchdowns and five interceptions, which works out to about 8.1 yards per attempt but a passer efficiency rating of 140.3 that ranks 12th in the ACC, one spot ahead of Duke's Walker Eget (139.9). The profile is a gambler's. Edwards is not afraid of the downfield throw, and Petrino's offense is built to give him those chances. His own description after Pitt was that "every play is designed to be an 80-yard touchdown," and the 75-yard seam throw to Shamar Easter that won the game was exactly that.

The cost of that approach shows up in two places. The first is turnovers: two interceptions at Pitt (one of which gave the Panthers the ball at the UNC 41, and the defense bailed him out with a fourth-down stop), two against Notre Dame, both of which turned directly into touchdowns, and one earlier in the year. The second is the sacks. Edwards has been taken down 11 times in five games, and Duke has 11 sacks of its own, so the matchup is a clear one: UNC's offensive line has to hold up better than its sack total suggests it can.

The positive trend is real, though. Edwards threw for 249 yards on 26 attempts at Pitt (9.6 yards per attempt), tied a career high with three touchdowns against Notre Dame, and has been unflustered in games where the run game gave him nothing. UNC has averaged 23.4 points a game with a rushing attack that totaled fewer than 100 yards over its last two games (69 against Notre Dame, 20 at Pitt by NCFootballNews's count) and the passing game has covered for it.

DukeWalker Eget is a sixth-year senior from San Jose State who has been through a surgically repaired knee and, plays best when he can step into his throws. He is 84 of 134 (62.7 percent) for 980 yards, seven touchdowns and just one interception, a 7:1 touchdown-to-interception ratio that is the best part of his profile. He has been hard to sack, taking just one in the first four games. His season has been a story in three acts: a shaky Duke debut against Tulane (13 of 26, 131 yards), a strong stretch against Illinois (15 of 21, 206 yards, three touchdowns), Stanford (14 of 24, 202 yards, one touchdown, one interception) and William & Mary (16 of 22, 182 yards, two touchdowns), and then an inconsistent day at Georgia Tech in wet conditions where he threw off his back foot and missed a few deep balls.

Eget is a game manager with a big arm and occasional accuracy lapses. If UNC can get him off his plant leg, he can be rattled. The corollary is that he does not need to be great for Duke to win. His job is to hit the check-down to Sheppard and the occasional shot to Jared Richardson or Jeremiah Hasley, and Diaz's offense is built around not asking much more of him. Backups are redshirt freshman Dan Mahan and true freshman Terry Walker III, who has thrown only four passes this year.

EdgeEven. Duke is a little more efficient and a little safer with the ball, UNC has the higher ceiling, and the two cancel out. Edwards has the higher ceiling and the lower floor.

North CarolinaThis is the group the UNC offense was supposed to build on and has stalled out. Benjamin Hall has emerged as the lead back with 55 carries for 276 yards (5.0 per carry) but no touchdowns, thanks to a breakout game at Clemson. Demon June has 37 carries and five touchdowns, most of them short-yardage, including the one-yard score at Pitt that gave UNC its first lead. Sophomore Jaylen McGill, who scored the first touchdown of the season against TCU on a nine-yard run, has one touchdown and 97 yards on 18 carries. As a room, UNC's backs have 543 yards on 118 carries (4.6 per carry) and six touchdowns, with June at 143 yards on 37 carries (3.9 per carry), so most of the room's efficiency comes from Hall and McGill. In the Pitt game the team as a whole managed 20 yards on 25 carries, and the Panthers' run defense came into the game allowing under 55 yards a game, so context matters.

The ground game was the identity of this team in the first three weeks (90 yards against TCU, a 35-3 win over East Tennessee State) and has disappeared against the two best defenses UNC has faced since. Duke allows 84.0 yards a game on the ground, third in the ACC behind only Miami and Pitt, and sixth nationally, so another quiet day is a reasonable baseline. Time of possession is the cost. UNC's offense is 132nd nationally in time of possession and it only gets worse when the running game does not produce.

DukeNate Sheppard is the story, and he deserves every superlative he gets. He is fourth in the FBS with 743 rushing yards, first in rushing yards per game (148.6) and first in all-purpose yards per game (180.8). He has scored in all five games, topped 100 rushing yards in four of five (99 at Georgia Tech, a game in which he also caught seven passes for 91 yards, both career highs, and finished with 190 yards from scrimmage and two touchdowns while playing through an ankle injury), and averages 6.3 yards a carry on 118 carries. He has 11 touchdowns (eight rushing, three receiving) and accounted for nine of Duke's first 19 rushing and receiving touchdowns through four games. He has 1,875 career rushing yards in two seasons and 54 career catches, including 17 this year for 161 yards. Belichick's assessment this week, "if you let Sheppard out, you're going to pay for it," is as honest as coaches get, and he added that Sheppard is very good at breaking tackles and making yards after contact.

The number to watch is the ankle. Sheppard did not look like himself in the second half of the Georgia Tech game in terms of explosiveness, and he is listed as questionable on Duke's initial availability report with an undisclosed injury. What I am hearing is that he plays, but I would not assume he is at full speed. Rutgers transfer CJ Campbell is the RB2 and gives Duke a legitimate change of pace, with 185 yards on 42 carries. As a room, Duke's backs have 1,061 yards on 182 carries (5.8 per carry) and 11 touchdowns, which is roughly double UNC's per-game output (212.2 yards a game to 108.6). For what it is worth, Duke's back Anderson Castle scored three touchdowns in this fixture last November.

The matchup problem is obvious. UNC's run defense is allowing 152.0 yards a game, has allowed 150 or more in each of its last three games, and has been worn down in the third quarter in particular. Sheppard will touch the ball 25 to 30 times. The question is whether the Tar Heels can hold him to something like 4 yards a carry with a few negative plays mixed in, or whether he breaks one early and the whole game plan changes.

EdgeDuke, and it is not close. Sheppard may be the best player on the field.

North CarolinaThis is the best position group on the roster and the reason the offense is much improved from 2025. Jordan Shipp, who has a case as the ACC's top receiver, has 26 catches for 366 yards (14.1 per catch), no touchdowns yet, and a surge over the last three games (19 catches, a career-high 109 yards against Notre Dame, 93 against Pitt on six catches). Receiver Keeyun Chapman is out and Mason Humphrey is questionable, and tight end Jordan Washington, who has yet to debut, is also questionable, though Deems May and Kymistrii Young are probable. Edwards and Shipp have a clear rapport, and most of Petrino's shots to the outside run through him. The absence of touchdowns is a quirk of how UNC has scored (short-yardage runs and tight-end targets), not a sign that the production is empty.

The tight end room is the more interesting story. Ohio State transfer Jelani Thurman (6-foot-6, 245 pounds, a redshirt junior) has 22 catches for 262 yards and a team-high four touchdowns, and he is the second-leading receiver. He had 94 yards against TCU in the opener (more than his entire 2025 at Ohio State), had two touchdown catches against Notre Dame, and scored again at Pitt. Five of his first 11 catches went for 20 or more yards. UNC's own players have gone out of their way to say that no linebacker in the country can stay in front of him, and the UNC defenders who face him in practice have said he is probably the best tight end they will see all year. Shamar Easter, a returner at the position, has 122 receiving yards and the biggest play of the season, the 75-yard touchdown that beat Pitt. Freshman Carson Sneed and returner Deems May round out a room that now has five new faces. UNC does use multiple tight ends: against TCU, Thurman played 55 snaps, Easter 12 and Sneed 10.

Diaz was asked this week about the key to stopping UNC's tight ends, and his defense has not yet seen a pair like Thurman and Easter.

DukeThe receiving corps is where Duke is not as deep as people assume. Penn transfer Jared Richardson was expected to be the WR1 after a decorated career at Penn (193 catches, 2,505 yards and 27 touchdowns, with a First Team FCS Football Central All-American season in 2025), but he did not log a catch in the first two games and has been a slower-than-advertised contributor, with four catches for 29 yards and a touchdown at Georgia Tech being a typical line. Tight end Jeremiah Hasley is the leading receiver with 19 catches for 234 yards, no touchdowns, and he was a major factor against UNC last year, when he caught a touchdown at Kenan Stadium. Idaho State transfer Jonah Burton, Kavon Simmons, Charlotte transfer Javen Nicholas and Jaivon Solomon round out the rotation, and five Blue Devils caught their first Duke touchdowns in the William & Mary game. Nate Kurisky has been a quiet contributor with two catches for 14 yards at Georgia Tech. Wideouts Jamien Little, Blake Rohrer and Qeanu Johnson are all listed out, and I have no read on how much each was playing, but a corps this short on proven options does not need to get smaller.

The pass-catchers are not scaring anyone on their own, and I think this is the most overlooked thing in the Duke profile. Sheppard (17 catches, 161 yards, three touchdowns) is effectively the No. 1 receiving threat, and when Eget struggled to connect downfield at Georgia Tech, his key completions on the last drive were check-downs to Sheppard.

EdgeNorth Carolina. Shipp and Thurman are the two best pass-catchers in this game, and I think that is a fair statement even accounting for Sheppard's role as a receiver.

North CarolinaUNC's offensive line has been a work in progress, but it has trended in the right direction. All five starters played all 57 snaps in the season opener against TCU, a feat of continuity for a group that was extensively rebuilt, and they did not draw a flag in Dublin. Left tackle Jordan Hall is listed as questionable. The group includes Arkansas transfer Jacqawn McRoy (a redshirt sophomore and former four-star recruit who began his career at Oregon) at right tackle, Charleston Southern transfer Andrew Threatt (a senior) at left guard, and Christo Kelly, who said this week that "a lot of plays" are "one block away" from getting the running game going. Aidan Banfield also saw action in the opener, and a missing left tackle would put more pressure on that depth.

The numbers tell a mixed story. Edwards has been sacked 11 times, UNC ranks 98th in rushing offense and generated 20 yards on 25 carries at Pitt, and 69 yards on the ground against Notre Dame. In its favor, pass protection held up well enough to let Edwards throw for 249 yards at Pitt even with at least one sack, UNC leads the ACC in discipline, and the line is clearly better than the 2025 version. But the schedule has taken a toll, since UNC has faced two of the better defensive fronts in the conference in a row. The group is a work in progress that stepped up at Pitt, and Duke's front does not look like Pitt's.

DukeThe offensive line is Duke's most reliable position group and the reason Sheppard's numbers are what they are. Cal transfer Braden Miller and Coastal Carolina transfer Del Grande are the two big newcomers (both day-one starters), with Matt Craycraft and Bradley Smith in the middle, and Eget was sacked just once in the first four games. Matt Craycraft is listed as questionable, and linemen Kai Jacobowitz, Reagan McCranie and Roman Fina are out, so the depth behind a healthy five is thin. The group opens lanes for a 226.6-yard rushing attack, though Eget did struggle against pressure in the Georgia Tech rain.

The matchup is the one Diaz was asked about this week: Duke's offensive line against UNC's defensive line. It is the most important matchup of the day, and it favors the offense if you believe the season-long numbers and favors the defense if you believe that the Tar Heels' front is the best unit on the team.

EdgeNarrowly Duke. Better continuity and better results, and a staff that knows how to scheme around the run, but the gap is smaller than the rushing numbers suggest, and Duke has questions behind Craycraft.

North CarolinaThis is UNC's best defensive group, and it is a pass-rush story. Xavier Lewis, a 6-foot, 250-to-260-pound defensive tackle who made his first career start in Dublin, leads the way with 6.5 sacks (sixth nationally) and 9.5 tackles for loss (fourth nationally, tied for second in TFLs per game at 1.9). He is an all-down player, he set a career high in tackles against Pitt and was named the ACC Defensive Lineman of the Week. Edge rusher Melkart Abou Jaoude missed two games with an injury and returned against Notre Dame, and Jaylen Harvey has been a disruptive complement. At full strength the line is one of the best and most complete units in the conference.

The caveat is that the unit has been less effective against the run in the second half of games, and it has had a pattern of being worn down. It is also short-handed: Viliami Moala, Vodney Cleveland and Jimmy-Phrisco Alo-Suliafu are out, Isaiah Johnson is questionable and Harvey is probable, which means the rotation behind Lewis is thinner than at any point this season. Notre Dame's 122 third-quarter rushing yards is the cleanest example.

DukeThe defensive line has the talent and has not quite delivered the results. The edge rushers are sophomore Bryce Davis and junior Tyshon Reed (6-foot-3, 245 pounds), and the production has not matched the profile. The most consistent criticism is the interior: Duke lacks an impact defensive tackle, with size and penetration problems that showed up at Georgia Tech, where the Yellow Jackets ran for 165 yards and running back Trelain Maddox scored three touchdowns. Duke has 11 sacks through five games and had zero against Illinois.

UNC's offensive line is not a great unit, but it does not need to be great against this front. The interior of Duke's defense is the weak link, and that is where UNC has to run between the tackles.

EdgeSlight North Carolina, because of Lewis and the pass rush. The run-stopping comparison is closer than the rankings suggest.

North CarolinaThe linebacker room returned essentially nobody from last season, which is why two transfers matter so much. Derek McDonald, a Syracuse transfer coming back from a season-ending hip injury, is the team's leading tackler with 49 tackles (20 solo) and had 12 against Pitt, including six solo stops. Peyton Seelmann, another transfer, is the other major piece, and Jonathan Agumadu is in the mix. McDonald and Lewis form one of the most formidable defensive duos in the ACC on the pass-rush and tackling side of the ledger.

The question for this group is the one the defense as a whole faces: run fits against a back who creates yards after contact. UNC has allowed 150 or more rushing yards in three straight games and has been hit hardest in the second half. Linebackers who get caught in traffic or take poor angles against Sheppard will turn four-yard runs into 14-yard runs. McDonald's experience is his best attribute here.

DukeThe linebacker group was projected as potentially the best in the ACC, and it has been good but not dominant. Luke Mergott, the hero of last year's ACC Championship game, and sixth-year veteran Nick Morris Jr. (back from a season-ending injury) anchor it, with Bradley Gompers also in the rotation. Mergott was inconsistent at Georgia Tech, with numerous missed tackles, and Gompers committed the costly late hit on the Yellow Jackets' game-winning drive. The group lost Elliott Schaper for the season this week (five tackles through four games), which matters for depth more than for production.

The group is physical and fast. Diaz said in July that Duke has "eleven guys running over twenty-two miles an hour," and the linebackers are a big part of that claim. They have been very good against the run, which is why the Blue Devils rank sixth nationally in rushing defense.

EdgeSlight North Carolina. Duke has the more proven group on paper, but McDonald-led UNC is more capable than its run numbers suggest, and Duke is without Schaper.

Where North Carolina Should Target

1

The tight ends against Duke's second level.

This is the cleanest mismatch on the board, and Diaz was asked about it directly this week. Thurman is 6-foot-6, 245 pounds, has 22 catches for 262 yards and a team-high four touchdowns, and has beaten linebackers and safeties all year. Easter has 122 yards and the 75-yard touchdown that beat Pitt. Duke has not struggled against tight ends so far this season, but it has not faced a duo that can both stretch the field and block. Seam routes, rollouts, and play-action off the zone-read are the shots. I want UNC to open with Thurman on the first drive the way it did against TCU, when two of his first catches went for 20 or more yards on the opening possession. Duke's linebackers had missed-tackle issues at Georgia Tech, and a tight end who finishes through contact gets a lot of that exposed.

2

Duke's interior defensive line, on the ground.

UNC has run for fewer than 100 yards over its last two games and ranks 98th nationally, and that has to be acknowledged. But the most consistent criticism of Duke's defense is the absence of an impact defensive tackle. Georgia Tech ran for 165 yards and Trelain Maddox scored three times. UNC does not need a big rushing number. It needs Hall to average 4 yards a carry on 15 to 18 attempts, mostly between the tackles, with June in short yardage, so that third downs are third-and-4 instead of third-and-9. UNC was 3 for 11 on third down at Pitt, and that is the statistic that mattered most, because it handed the ball back to Pitt for 36:32.

3

Duke's penalties, and the field position they give away.

Duke is 122nd in fewest penalties, 126th in penalties per game, and committed six for 71 yards in Atlanta. UNC is tied for fifth nationally in fewest penalties and is among the most disciplined teams in the ACC. It is a straightforward edge: if both teams take care of the football otherwise, Duke is going to give UNC two or three free first downs or half-the-distance flags. The Tar Heels do not have the offense to waste those drives, but they have the discipline to convert them.

4

Explosive plays over the top against a rebuilt secondary.

Edwards has been, by his own description, hunting the 80-yard touchdown, and Duke's secondary lost Rivers, Moore and Weaver and is starting a mix of transfers. Duke has also generated one interception in five games, and the player who made it, Evan Smith, is questionable. That means Edwards can take shots, particularly to Shipp on the outside, with a lower risk of the ball being taken away than he has faced against Notre Dame and Pitt. The caveat is that I would rather he take the shots on early downs and in the first half, and do not force them when the pocket breaks down.

5

The first half, because Duke's second-half defense is the real problem.

Duke outscored opponents 62-6 in the second half of its first four games, which points to the Blue Devils being a team that closes. UNC should therefore treat the first half as the period to build a lead. The Tar Heels scored on their opening drive against Notre Dame and led 17-6 at halftime at Clemson, and the fourth quarter has been the problem in both of the last two Duke games. A 14-point halftime lead would not be safe, but it would force Duke to play from behind, and Duke is more dangerous in a close, grinding game than in one where Eget has to throw.

6

Red-zone touchdowns and field-goal pressure.

Both defenses rank in the top 25 nationally in red-zone touchdowns allowed, so points are going to be at a premium. UNC needs touchdowns, not field goals, and it should use fourth-and-short aggressively inside the 10. The Tar Heels showed against Notre Dame what a goal-line stand can do, stopping the Irish on fourth down at the 3-yard line and then driving 97 yards the other way. Conversely, Duke's new kicker means that Duke's field-goal drives are less automatic than they were a week ago.

Where North Carolina Should Be Concerned

1

Nate Sheppard and the run defense.

There is no more important sentence in this preview. UNC has allowed 150 or more rushing yards in three straight games, 152.0 a game overall, ranks 88th nationally against the run, and was worn down in the third quarter at Notre Dame (122 yards on 20 carries). Sheppard averages 148.6 rushing yards a game and 180.8 all-purpose yards, has scored in every game, and is a better receiver than most running backs. He is listed as questionable with an undisclosed injury, and the ankle he hurt at Georgia Tech may limit his top-end speed. I would not count on either. If Sheppard goes for 130-plus on the ground and 40-plus in the air, UNC is probably losing, because the Tar Heels' offense does not have the possessions to keep up.

2

Time of possession and the math it creates.

Duke holds the ball 33:40 a game (eighth nationally), UNC 26:40 (132nd). At Pitt it was 36:32 to 23:28 and UNC still won, which is the best argument for the Tar Heels' resilience, but it is not a sustainable formula. A long, grinding Duke drive in the second half is how Notre Dame beat UNC three weeks ago. UNC's defense has been stronger in short bursts than in long sequences, and Duke's offense is built around the long sequence. The Blue Devils are 114th in seconds per play, which also means there will be fewer possessions, and fewer possessions favor the team with the lead.

3

Edwards' turnovers and Duke's pass rush.

Edwards has thrown five interceptions, four of them in the last two games, and has been sacked 11 times. Notre Dame turned two of his interceptions directly into touchdowns, and the one at Pitt gave the Panthers the ball at the UNC 41. UNC has surrendered the ball eight times overall. Duke has 11 sacks but only one interception on the season, so the Blue Devils are not a ball-hawking defense, but a short field after a sack-fumble or a tipped ball is the kind of play that changes a three-point game. Turnover margin is the one variable that would swing this game by itself.

4

The offense's third-down and rushing profile.

The Tar Heels were 3 for 11 on third down at Pitt, managed 20 rushing yards, and still won because a 75-yard touchdown covered for everything else. That will not happen every week. Against the No. 6 run defense nationally, UNC has to believe it can win through the air without being one-dimensional, and the offensive line has to prove that Pitt was a sign of progress rather than a one-week exception.

5

The secondary and the thin defensive line, now confirmed by the report.

Tre Miller is out, Kaleb Cost is doubtful, Jakob Weatherspoon is out and Greg Smith III is questionable. Duke's passing numbers are modest, but Eget does his best work off play-action, and a patched-together back end is where that gets exposed. The defensive line is short too, with Moala, Cleveland and Alo-Suliafu out and Isaiah Johnson questionable, and left tackle Jordan Hall is questionable on the other side of the ball. Watch Thursday night's update and the final report two hours before kickoff, because Smith's status is the one that changes my read the most.

6

The late-game history and the venue.

North Carolina has led in both of the last two meetings and lost both, after leading 20-0 in 2024 and taking a one-point lead in the fourth quarter last year before Duke scored with two minutes left. Diaz has shown he is willing to be aggressive, as the fake field goal last November demonstrated, and The Blue Devils are also coming home off a road loss with plenty of motivation. A team that has beaten UNC twice in a row, in the last minutes both times, is not going to be intimidated by being an underdog in the second half.

7

The slow-start pattern.

Belichick's defense has struggled on the first couple of drives before settling in and limiting the opposing offense. Duke has a running back who excels in a rhythm. A 10-0 deficit is survivable. A 17-0 deficit against a team that outscored its first four opponents 62-6 after halftime is not.

How I Think the Game Breaks Down

The shape of this game is set by one question: can UNC score enough in the first 30 minutes to make Duke play a way it does not want to play?

Duke will take the opening possession and try to establish Sheppard, and I expect the first two Duke drives to work, because that is the pattern for Belichick's defense and because Sheppard's tackle-breaking is hardest to stop when a defense is still finding its fits. Call it a touchdown and a field goal on the first two possessions, with Duke holding the ball for most of the first quarter. UNC needs to answer immediately, and the way is the tight end. I expect Petrino to script a handful of Thurman and Easter touches early, a seam, a rollout, a screen to Shipp off the same look, and for Edwards to get one of those for a touchdown late in the first quarter or early in the second. Halftime: Duke 10, UNC 7, or something close to it, with Duke having run more plays but UNC having generated more yards per play.

The third quarter is where the Notre Dame film would worry me and the Pitt film would encourage me. The Tar Heels' defense gave up 122 rushing yards on 20 carries in the third quarter at Notre Dame, but held Pitt to 17 points even though the Panthers held the ball for 36:32 and UNC was 3 for 11 on third down. The Tar Heels do not have to be good for 60 minutes. They have to be good at the moments the game turns. I expect Duke to score once more in the third quarter, Sheppard in the red zone, to make it 17-7 or 17-10, and I expect UNC to respond with the kind of play it made at Pitt, when the 75-yard seam throw to Easter decided the game: a downfield shot to Shipp, or a Thurman or Easter catch-and-run, that turns a two-possession deficit into a one-possession game.

The fourth quarter is the part I trust the least and the part the whole prediction depends on. Duke has won each of the last two meetings late, 21-20 after trailing 20-0 and 32-25 on a go-ahead touchdown with 2:18 left that followed a fake field goal. In both games the winner was the team willing to take a risk at the key moment, and Diaz is a coach who has shown he will. What I would expect is a final 15 minutes where UNC holds a narrow lead, Duke runs a long drive to cut it, and the Tar Heels' defense needs a stop in the red zone or a turnover on downs. The Pitt game, where the defense kept bailing out an offense that could not hold the ball, is the template. The margin Saturday is likely to be a missed kick, a flag, or a single third down.

Three things would change this picture quickly. If Edwards throws a pick that turns into a Duke touchdown, the Tar Heels are probably chasing from two scores down, and I do not think this offense can win that way against a Duke team that is 8th in time of possession. If Sheppard is healthy and breaks a long run in the first quarter, it will be a Duke afternoon. The injury report has already made the secondary the weak point, with Miller out and Cost doubtful, and if Greg Smith III sits as well it is worse than I am assuming here. On the other side, one fortunate bounce, a Duke penalty on a third down, a missed kick by Gavin Strange, would be enough to tip a three-point game.

The market has Duke -3.5 and UNC's moneyline at +128, which implies UNC wins roughly 44 percent of the time. I think that is a fair number, and my lean toward UNC is a lean, not a certainty. The Tar Heels have played the tougher schedule (10th nationally, against Duke's outside the top 90), they are the more disciplined team, they have the better pass-catchers and the best individual defender in this game in Lewis, and they have a kicker advantage. Duke has the best player on the field (assuming he plays), the better offensive line, the better rushing numbers on both sides of the ball, and two straight wins in this series.
